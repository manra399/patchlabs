Security risks of vibe-coded apps, with data

The most serious risk PatchLabs has measured in vibe-coded apps is a database the wrong people can read. In the PatchLabs lab study (Nov 2026) of 318 public Lovable and Supabase apps, 106 (33%) did: 70 were readable by anyone with the public key and 36 by anyone who signs up. After removing businesses’ own public contact details, about 1 in 4 (25–28%) exposed real personal data.

By Manraj Singh Dhillon, technical founderChecked 11 Nov 20264 min read

TL;DR

  • The measured risk is data exposure: 106 of 318 apps (33%), about 1 in 4 after removing false alarms.
  • The cause is a handful of access-rule patterns, each with a finding page: RLS never switched on, read rules that say yes to everyone, and read rules that only check “signed in”.
  • Other common risks are secret keys in code, open server functions, self-made admins and weak HTTPS. We list them with finding IDs but have no study numbers for them.
  • Every risk here can be checked with a free tool or a short test.

How common are security flaws in vibe-coded apps?

PatchLabs sampled 500 public GitHub repos with a Lovable and Supabase project layout, and rebuilt each app’s database from its own migrations in a disposable lab. We then read the copy as an anonymous visitor and as a signed-in stranger who owns none of the rows. Here is what came out.

StageAppsShare of 318
Public repos sampled500n/a
Run through the lab436n/a
Migrations built a working database318100%
Personal data readable by the wrong people10633%
…by anyone with the public key7022%
…only by people who sign up3611%
Real exposure after removing businesses’ own public contact detailsabout 1 in 425–28%

Source: PatchLabs lab study, Nov 2026. The full report has the data and methodology. The 118 repos that did not build are excluded, not counted as safe.

Limits. The study used public Lovable and Supabase repos only, and tested read access to personal data. It says nothing about private repos, other stacks or other kinds of flaw, and it does not show that these apps are worse than hand-written ones. No app is named.

Why do AI app builders produce these flaws?

An AI builder aims to make the app work first. When a page fails with a permissions error, the quickest way to clear it is to loosen a rule, and a loosened rule can open the whole table. The study also found that leaks are not limited to the users table. Apps exposed the people their users deal with, such as suppliers, staff and sellers, and logs of private conversations.

A second pattern is a third of the leaks needing a sign-up. If an app lets anyone create an account, “signed in” is not a boundary, and tests run only as a logged-out visitor miss those 36 apps.

How do I find out which risks apply to my app?

  1. Run the free RLS check on a public repo. It reads the migrations and flags tables that are open (risks 1 to 4).
  2. Paste your code into the key check to find secret keys (risk 6). It runs in your browser.
  3. Grade your live home page with the headers check (risk 12).
  4. Test as a stranger. Follow the three-identity script or the 10-minute Lovable check.
  5. Work through the pre-launch checklist for the rest.

Questions people ask

What is the biggest security risk in a vibe-coded app?

In PatchLabs’ lab study, it was data exposure. 106 of 318 public Lovable and Supabase apps (33%) let the wrong people read personal data, and about 1 in 4 exposed real personal data once false alarms were removed.

Are vibe-coded apps less secure than hand-written ones?

We cannot say. The study sampled public Lovable and Supabase repos and did not compare them with hand-written apps. It shows how often this stack leaks, not how it ranks against others.

Why does “signed in” not mean “safe”?

If anyone can create an account, a stranger can sign up with a throwaway email. In the study, 36 of 318 apps leaked only to signed-in users. See PL-002.

Does a green security scan mean my app is safe?

It means the checks that scan runs found nothing. Read builder scan vs independent check for what a scan can and cannot tell you.

Does PatchLabs name the apps it found leaking?

No. The study used public code only, no app is named, and nothing here implies that any real app, company or repo has a flaw.
